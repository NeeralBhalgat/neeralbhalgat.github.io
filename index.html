Project 0

Becoming Friends with Your Camera

Exploring how camera position and focal length change perspective, apparent depth, and the relationship between a subject and its background.

Part 1

Selfie: The Wrong Way vs. The Right Way

The first image was taken very close to the face. For the second image, I stepped several feet back and zoomed in so the face remained roughly the same size in the frame.

Intuition

Perspective is controlled primarily by camera position, not by zoom itself. When the camera is very close, the nose is much closer to the lens than the ears and sides of the face, so those depth differences become exaggerated. Moving farther away reduces the relative depth differences, and zooming in simply restores the face to a similar size in the image.

Part 2

Architectural Perspective Compression

I first photographed the building from farther away while zooming in, then moved closer and used a wider field of view while keeping the building at approximately the same scale in the frame.

Intuition

The apparent perspective changes because the camera position changes. From farther away, differences in distance between nearby and distant parts of the scene are small relative to the overall camera distance, so the scene looks flatter and more compressed. Moving closer increases those relative distance differences, which makes depth appear stronger.

Part 3

The Dolly Zoom

I moved the camera backward while zooming in, taking seven still images while keeping the wooden bird approximately the same size in frame.

Seven still photographs aligned and cropped into a smoothly looping animation.

Intuition

As the camera moves backward, perspective becomes flatter because the relative distance differences between the subject and background shrink. Zooming in compensates for the subject getting smaller in the frame, so the bird stays roughly fixed while the background appears to stretch and compress around it.
